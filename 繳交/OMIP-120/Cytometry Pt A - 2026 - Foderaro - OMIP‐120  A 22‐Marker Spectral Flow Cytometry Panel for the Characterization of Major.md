## **ABSTRACT** 

Tumors that develop in the bone exhibit poor responses to most treatment options, including advanced immunotherapies. Despite its immune- rich composition, the bone marrow microenvironment often fails to suppress tumor progression and instead promotes tumor growth and immune evasion, although the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. To investigate tumor- induced remodeling of the bone marrow immune environment and characterize tumor- infiltrating immune populations, we developed a high- dimensional 22- marker spectral flow cytometry panel for use in preclinical models of bone and bone marrow cancers. The panel enables profiling of major immune populations including CD4<sup>+</sup> T and CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells, B cells, Natural Killer cells, dendritic cells, monocytes, macrophages and neutrophils, and incorporates activation, exhaustion and immune checkpoint markers. Panel optimization was performed using spleen, freshly isolated bone marrow and tibial osteosarcoma tissue from adult BALB/c mice. While optimized for murine osteosarcoma and bone marrow tissue, this panel provides a robust and adaptable platform for high- dimensional immune profiling across solid tumors and hematological malignancies. 

## **1   |   Panel Presentation** 

## **1.1   |   Purpose and Appropriate Sample Types** 

We developed a high- dimensional 22- parameter spectral flow cytometry panel to investigate tumor- infiltrating immune cells and tumor- induced alterations in the bone marrow immune environment in murine bone cancer. The panel enables quantification of CD4<sup>+</sup> T cells, CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells, B cells, natural killer cells, dendritic cells, monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils. For deeper functional characterization, it includes activation, exhaustion, and immune checkpoint markers. Optimization was carried out in a syngeneic orthotopic osteosarcoma mouse 

model, utilizing spleen, freshly isolated bone marrow, and tibial osteosarcoma tissue from adult BALB/c mice. Although optimized for osteosarcoma tissue and bone marrow, the panel provides a robust backbone for high- dimensional immune profiling across various solid tumors and hematological malignancies.

interactions most often result in immunosuppression, a state in which the tumor- killing capacity of effector cells is reduced or lost entirely [2]. Immunotherapies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), aim at enhancing the ability of immune cells to recognize and eliminate tumor cells [3] and their outstanding efficacy has led to approval for multiple cancer types [4]. 

Osteosarcoma is characterized by a complex TME due to its natural primary site. Even under physiological conditions, the bone harbors a high percentage of immune regulatory cells and growth factors such as TGFβ and it is thus more prone to subversion by tumors arising in this context, including osteosarcomas and multiple myelomas, as well as metastatic tumors of epithelial origin [5, 6]. Under tumor influence, this immune- rich environment acquires the paradoxical characteristic of an overall tumor- supportive milieu, mainly composed of myeloid- derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), tumor- associated macrophages (TAMs) and regulatory T cells, and where cytotoxic T cells are typically underrepresented and display an exhausted phenotype [7]. These features render osteosarcoma resistant to ICIs and other advanced treatments, which explains the lack of approved novel therapies in the last decades [8]. Furthermore, bone metastases from different tumors are recognized as a site of resistance to immunotherapies, highlighting the bone marrow TME as a distinct niche that warrants in- depth characterization [9]. In this scenario, a fundamental step toward the design of novel rational immune treatments lies in the capacity of resolving immune cell phenotypes and tumor- induced changes using appropriate orthotopic in vivo models. 

With this in mind, we designed a 22- marker spectral flow cytometry panel for a 4 Laser (UV- V- B- R) Aurora that identifies all major leukocyte subsets and functionally classifies them via a selection of activation, exhaustion and immune checkpoint markers. The panel was validated on murine osteosarcoma samples, bone marrow and spleen (Tables 1 and 2).

This population can include polymorphonuclear myeloid- derived suppressor cells (PMN- MDSCs) [19]. From the neutrophil gate, we plotted CD206 to identify N2 neutrophils [20]; these populations can be further characterized by the expression level of PD- L1 [21, 22]. From the remaining CD11b<sup>+</sup> cells, we plotted Ly6C against F4/80, from which we identified Ly6C “high” (classical/inflammatory) and Ly6C “low” (nonclassical/patrolling/anti- inflammatory) monocytes and F4/80<sup>+</sup> macrophages [23, 24]. As for neutrophils, currently it is not possible to distinguish between monocytes and monocytic myeloid- derived suppressor cells (M- MDSCs) in mice [19]. In addition, monocytes are known to express varying levels of F4/80 [25–27] and Ly6C can be expressed at different levels by macrophages [28, 29]. Monocytes and macrophages can be further investigated for immune checkpoint and activation marker expression [30, 31]. Based on the expression levels of MHCII [32, 33] and CD206 [34] macrophages can be classified in M1 (CD206<sup>−</sup> / MHCII<sup>+</sup> ) and M2 (CD206<sup>+</sup> ). Monocytes can be investigated for markers of activation such as CD86 [35] and PD- L1 [31]. From the CD11b<sup>−</sup> gate, we excluded F4/80<sup>+</sup> Ly6C<sup>+</sup> cells and plotted TCRβ against CD19, identifying B cells. These can be further classified based on MHCII and PD- L1 expression levels [36, 37].

While other murine immune profiling panels have characterized tumor microenvironments in solid tumors such as colon cancer, lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, and glioblastoma [38–40], we present the first 22- color spectral flow cytometry panel optimized for the osteosarcoma tumor microenvironments. ## **4   |   Similarity to Other OMIPS** 

The panel described is partly overlapping with OMIP- 032 [41], which is focused on the innate and adaptive immune subsets in mouse organs. The common markers are Ly6G, Ly6C, CD8β, NKp46, MHCII, CD4, CD11c, CD49b, CD11b, TCRβ, CD206, CD45. OMIP- 054 [38], designed for immune phenotyping of innate and adaptive leukocytes in the brain, spleen, and bone marrow of mice, shows partial overlapping markers (CD45, 

TCRβ, CD4, FoxP3, CD8β, Ly6G, Ly6C, CD11b, CD11c, MHCII, CD206, CD69, CTLA- 4, PD- 1, PD- L1, and TIM- 3). OMIP- 61 [42] shows a limited number of overlapping markers (CD45, CD3, Ly6G, F4/80, CD11b, CD11c, and Ly6C) and is designed for the characterization of murine antigen- presenting cells. All OMIPs mentioned above are designed and optimized for mass cytometry or conventional flow cytometry. OMIP- 086 [46] is a spectral flow cytometry panel designed for the characterization of mouse innate lymphoid cell subsets and has partial overlapping markers (CD45, NKp46, CD49b, CD4, CD11b, CD11c, CD19, F4/80, and TCRβ). ## **6   |   Animal Experiments and Bone Marrow Isolation**

## **8   |   Panel Design and Optimization** 

## **8.1   |   Panel Design** 

To assign fluorochromes to the selected markers in our panel, we followed a combination of well- established panel design guidelines [50, 52–54], namely: 

- Limiting overall similarities between fluorochrome emission spectra; 

- Preferentially assigning bright fluorochromes to weakly expressed markers and dim fluorochromes to highly expressed markers; 

- Assigning co- expressed markers to fluorochromes with limited spectral overlap; 

- Limiting Spillover Spread values. 

Furthermore, we took into consideration that autofluorescence (AF) is generally more prominently detected in the violet and ultraviolet channels. To avoid loss of resolution or false positives arising from AF signals, we assigned markers for lymphoid cells to these channels, while pairing myeloid markers with fluorochromes that emit predominantly in the red channels. Figure 2 shows the final combination of fluorochromes in the panel. To assess similarities between fluorochromes, we assessed the cosine similarity index, a metric that compares fluorochrome emission spectra represented as vectors returning values ranging from 0 (no similarities) to 1 (identical spectra). As shown in Figure 3, all fluorochrome pairs in our final panel exhibited cosine similarity indices lower than 0.89, which resulted in a low condition number (8.9), reflecting low interference among the fluorochromes in the panel.

### **8.2.5** | **Single Stained Control Versus Multicolor Resolution for Each Marker** 

To validate the panel performance, we compared the marker resolution in the single stained reference controls to that in the multicolor sample. This test allows us to check whether any loss of resolution occurs when all the antibodies are combined and unmixed (multicolor sample). The test was performed on splenocytes or bone marrow cells processed immediately after isolation from the same mouse, stained in parallel for single stained controls and multicolor sample, and gated on single cells. Since the single- stain controls contained fewer events than the multicolor samples, we subsampled the multicolor data for visualization purposes to match the number of events in the single- stain controls. As shown in Figure 12, the marker resolution between the single stained and multicolor sample was generally preserved. In some cases (e.g., CD11c, F4/80, Ly6G, NKp46, TIM- 3), a reduction in resolution was observed. Importantly, this did not affect the identification of relevant subsets or the percentage of positive cells. As such, no further changes in antibody titers or staining protocol were made. 

## **9   |   Epitope Sensitivity to Tissue Dissociation Protocols** 

Enzymatic dissociation of tissues can cleave cell surface markers, thereby impairing flow cytometry downstream analysis. To assess and minimize this effect for tumor tissue immunophenotyping, we treated spleens with two different enzymatic digestion protocols (A and B) and compared marker expression 

to that of mashed spleens, our standard spleen dissociation protocol. With protocol A the tissues were cut in pieces of 1 × 1 mm and placed in a tube together with 3 mL of digestion medium which consisted of 0.2 mg/mL DNase I, 1 mg/mL Collagenase A and 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in RPMI 1640 without HEPES. The tubes were placed at 37°C for 45 min and gently inverted every 5 min. Next, the supernatant was collected, and the digestion process was repeated a total of three times. All the supernatant was put through a 100 μm filter and centrifuged at 530 _g_ for 5 min. Pellet was resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer (2% FBS in PBS). For protocol B [60] the tissues were cut in pieces of 1 × 1 mm and placed in a tube together with 3 mL of digestion medium which consisted of 0.1 mg/mL DNase I, 0.2 mg/mL Collagenase P, 0.8 mg/mL Dispase in RPMI 1640 without HEPES. The tubes were placed at 37°C for 30 min and gently inverted every 5 min. The mixture was then placed in the waterbath and fragments were allowed to settle for 30 s, after which the enzyme mix was removed, added to 10 mL of ice- cold flow cytometry staining buffer and centrifuged at 300 _g_ for 4 min. Two mL of fresh enzyme mix was added to the digestion tubes and mixed gently with a 1 mL pipet followed by 10 min incubation while regularly gently mixing with a 1 mL pipet. Next, the suspension was mixed vigorously with a 1 mL pipet, and fragments were allowed to settle. The supernatant was added to the previous spun cell pellet and fresh enzyme mix was added and mixed vigorously with a 1 mL pipet every 5 min until a clear solution was obtained. Supernatants were centrifuged after each removal (300 _g_ , 4 min, 4°C). Cells were filtered through a 100 μm filter and resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer. For the nonenzymatic dissociation protocol, a 100 μm strainer was placed on top of a 50 mL tube and prewet with RPMI + 10% FBS + 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin (p/s). Spleens were placed on the strainer and mashed using the plunger end of a syringe (1 mL) by making grinding circular movements to homogenize the tissue. The strainer was rinsed in between with RPMI + 10% FBS + 1% p/s to elute the cells from the strainer. This process was continued until only connective tissue was left on the strainer membrane. Then the strainer was rinsed again with RPMI + 10% FBS + 1% p/s and the tube was filled up until a total volume of 35 mL. Figure 13 shows the differences between the different protocols. Treatment with protocol B reduced expression of CD19 and CD4 compared to protocol A and mashed spleen. Furthermore, as reported in OMIP- 082 [61], Collagenase P, present in protocol B reduced resolution of NKp46. Given these results, we selected protocol A for the digestion of the tumor tissue.
