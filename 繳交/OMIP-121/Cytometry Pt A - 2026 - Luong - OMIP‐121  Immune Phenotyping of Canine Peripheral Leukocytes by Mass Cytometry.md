## **ABSTRACT** 

Dogs are a critical non- rodent species used in preclinical safety studies, particularly, in the pharmaceutical field, due to their physiological, metabolic, and immunological similarities to humans. As such, immunophenotyping of canine peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) plays a crucial role in translational research, immune monitoring, and safety evaluations in drug development. However, the limited availability of canine- specific antibodies restricts detailed and accurate immune profiling, which is essential for advancing safety evaluations in drug development. To address this challenge, we developed a 15- marker panel for comprehensive mass cytometry- based immunophenotyping of cryopreserved canine PBMCs. This panel encompasses major leukocyte subsets, including B cells, CD4+ T helper cells, regulatory T cells, CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, memory T cell subsets, natural killer T cells, natural killer cells, dendritic cells, CD4+ monocytes, classical monocytes, and neutrophils. We utilized both extracellular and intracellular markers to facilitate in- depth immune profiling, despite the limited availability of caninespecific antibodies. The panel was thoroughly optimized in terms of marker selection, antibody clone validation, and metal isotope pairing. Additionally, by the use of mass cytometry, several channels remain unoccupied, providing flexibility for future panel expansion. 

## **1   |   Panel Presentation** 

## **1.1   |   Purpose and Appropriate Sample Types** 

The canine immune system exhibits several parallels with the human immune system, enhancing the translatability of immune profiling data from dogs to humans [1]. Insights gained from canine immunophenotyping would contribute to improving the effectiveness of comparative oncology by facilitating a deeper understanding of cancer biology and supporting the development of improved diagnostics and therapies applicable to both species. One of the key approaches for understanding immune responses in such contexts is the immunophenotyping of PBMCs. Mass cytometry enables high- dimensional analysis of immune cell populations, providing a comprehensive and precise characterization 

at the single- cell level [2]. Previous advances—such as multicolor flow panels—have demonstrated the value of comprehensive immune profiling in dogs, while also highlighting the need for flexible, robust solutions for detailed leukocyte analysis [3, 4]. The implementation of mass cytometry allows the generation of a backbone panel, to which additional markers can be added without extensive panel design or modification of the backbone itself. Therefore, we developed a 15- marker mass cytometry panel designed for robust immunophenotyping of cryopreserved canine PBMCs (Tables 1 and 2). This panel enables the identification of multiple immune cell lineages, including B cells, CD4+ T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), CD8+ T cells, natural killer T (NKT) cells, natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells (DCs), CD4+ monocytes, classical monocytes, and neutrophils. To characterize T cell subsets, CD4 and CD8 were employed to enable the identification of helper and cytotoxic T cells, respectively. Tregs were defined by co- expression of CD25 and FOXP3 [7]. To characterize the differentiation status of T cells, we employed Eomes/CD44 phenotypic paradigms to define naïve (CD44−Eomes−), central memory (CD44 + Eomes−), and effector memory (CD44 + Eomes+) T cell subsets [8–11]. Eomes, a T- box transcription factor best known for its role in cytotoxic lymphocyte differentiation [9, 12, 13], but which also exerts important functions in CD4+ T cell differentiation [8, 11]. Eomes is transcriptionally upregulated during T cell activation and effector differentiation, with highest expression in effector memory CD8+ T cells and long- lived memory precursors, distinguishing them from naïve or central memory cells that express lower levels [9, 13]. In parallel, CD44 is a well- established marker of antigen- experienced cells and has been used to identify memory T cells in dogs [10, 14]. CD44 is upregulated following antigenic/mitogenic activation and remains elevated on memory CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, which produce higher levels of effector cytokines such as TNF- α compared with CD44- low naïve cells [4, 14]. For the identification of B cells, we employed a combination of CD21, CD22, and MHC- II markers, which together provide a robust definition of the peripheral B cells [15]. Mass cytometry is a powerful tool for high- dimensional and high- throughput single- cell analysis, enabling the simultaneous measurement of up to 60 parameters within a single panel [2]. In addition to the availability of preconjugated antibodies, the pairing of antibodies with appropriate metal isotopes requires careful consideration to ensure optimal signal intensity while minimizing signal interference. Antibodies targeting low- abundance markers should be conjugated to isotopes within the instrument's highsensitivity detection range, whereas highly abundant markers can be assigned to less sensitive channels [25]. Although signal spillover is substantially reduced in mass cytometry compared to fluorescent- based methods, three primary sources of interference must be addressed: _M_ ± 1 abundance spillover, _M_ + 16 oxidation spillover, and isotope impurity [2]. To minimize these effects, lowabundance markers should not be assigned to the _M_ ± 1 or _M_ + 16 channels of a highly expressed marker. Detailed steps of panel optimization are provided in Section 3. 

## **1.3   |   Similarity to Other Published OMIPS** 

To date, only one OMIP has focused on immunophenotyping of canine PBMCs and in- depth analysis of T- cell responses [3]. That panel comprised 13 markers and was designed for conventional flow cytometry. Our 15- marker panel represents the first mass cytometry- based OMIP designed for the comprehensive characterization of leukocyte subsets within canine PBMCs. While there is partial overlap with OMIP- 065, which enables profiling of B cells, CD4 helper T cells, CD8 cytotoxic T cells, Tregs, NK cells, neutrophils, and monocyte subsets, our panel is optimized for the additional characterization of DCs, NKT cells, activated NK cells, and memory T cell subsets.

A comprehensive literature review was conducted to identify published studies on canine immunophenotyping and vendor websites for the most current dog- specific and cross- reactive antibodies available commercially [26]. Within the T cell population, CD94 was subsequently used to distinguish NKT cells from conventional T cells, as anti- CD94 monoclonal Ab has been demonstrated to effectively enrich NK/NKT cell populations from PBMCs [6]. This finding is consistent with our analysis, in which CD94 expression was restricted to the NK cell and NKT cell subsets and absent from all other leukocyte populations (Figure 3A). Our analysis revealed that the major subset of NKT cells is composed of CD8+ NKT cells (Figure 3A). T cell subsets were further defined based on CD4 and CD8 expression, enabling the identification of helper and cytotoxic T cell populations. Notably, canine T cells also express MHC- II (Figure 3B). CD25 is also considered one of the functionally most important T cell activation markers [27]. Compared to CD4+ and CD8+ sp. T cells, dp T cells showed reduced frequencies of naïve cells and increased frequencies of central- memory and effector- memory T cells (Figure 3F). Notably, dp T cells also contain a subset of CD25 + FOXP3+ regulatory T cells, suggesting considerable heterogeneity within this population (Figure 3G) [10]. 

Canine NK cells are phenotypically defined as CD3- CD21GranzymeB+CD94+, and can be further subdivided into CD335+ and CD335− subsets, which are proposed to represent different activation stages [6, 17, 18, 20, 30]. CD335 (also known as NKp46 or NCR1) functions as an activating receptor on canine NK cells, initiating cytotoxic responses upon ligand engagement. CD335+ NK cells show spontaneous cytotoxicity against tumor targets like osteosarcoma cells in calcein release assays, which supports its use as a marker of functional maturity [18]. Moreover, the CD335+ subset expressed higher baseline levels of NK- associated genes (e.g., granzyme B, perforin) and transcription factors like GATA3, reflecting an activated state, while CD335− cells represent immature precursors that upregulate CD335 upon IL- 2 stimulation or expansion [20]. Both subsets display comparable cytokine secretion profiles poststimulation [18]. In the present study, the CD335+ subset has been shown to exhibit higher cytotoxic activity compared to the CD335− subset (Figure 4A). Additionally, CD8 expression is frequently observed on putative canine NK cells, with the expression in some conditions reportedly encompassing as much as 30% of the population (Figure 4B) [18, 19]. Interestingly, all CD8+ NK cells identified lacked CD335 expression, suggesting potential functional or developmental distinctions within the NK cell compartment (Figure 4B).

Interestingly, dogs are unique among mammals in exhibiting CD4 expression on peripheral blood neutrophils [24] (Figure 4D).

## **3.3   |   Data Preprocessing, Controls and Other Technical Considerations** 

### **3.3.1   |   Data Clean up, Normalization and Analysis** 

Following data acquisition, EQ Six Element Calibration Beads were utilized for normalization, and standardized FCS files were generated for downstream analysis. First, cells were identified by gating out the EQ Six Element Calibration Beads. Next, cell events were gated on any marker versus time to ensure data quality and consistency. Singlets were then gated using a bivariate plot of the DNA intercalator versus monocyte marker CD14, as monocytes exhibit elevated nucleic acid content, resulting in increased intercalator uptake. Gating based on the<sup>191</sup> Ir and<sup>193</sup> Ir axis may partially exclude monocytes [44]. Subsequently, live cells were identified by gating out<sup>195</sup> Pt positive cells. CD45<sup>141</sup> Pr were then used to identify leukocyte identity (Figure 9). Finally, we manually gated our desired lineage population (Table 5). The overall lineage gating strategy on CyTOFXT is illustrated in Figure 1. The full gating strategy on the Lunarion instrument is shown in Figure 10. Mass cytometry data were exported as FCS files using ArcSinh transformation with a cofactor of 4 for CyTOF XT and a cofactor of 5 for Lunarion. Analysis of mass cytometry data was carried out using FlowJo v10.10. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10. 

A comparison of reported leukocyte subsets frequencies within live single CD45+ cells, as assessed by both the CyTOF XT and Lunarion instruments, is illustrated in Figure 11A. Agreement between CyTOF XT and Lunarion was assessed by Bland–Altman analysis (Figure 11B). In general, Bland– Altman analysis demonstrated excellent agreement between CyTOF XT and Lunarion across leukocyte subsets (mean bias = −0.0714%, 95% limits of agreement = [−1.356%, 1.213%]), with the exception of central memory and naïve CD4+ T cell subsets. Greater variability in these two subset frequencies reflects their adjacent position in the bivariate plot of secondary markers (Eomes and CD44), where small threshold 

adjustments cause reciprocal shifts in measured frequencies [45]. However, this cross- platform comparison is based on a limited sample size ( _n_ = 3), which constrains the strength of the conclusions. With only three biological replicates, the estimated bias and limits of agreement should be interpreted as preliminary, and may not fully capture inter- individual variability or rare sources of technical discordance between instruments. Consequently, the current data support the notion of good agreement between CyTOF XT and Lunarion for the leukocyte subsets examined, but more extensive validation in a larger cohort would be required to robustly establish crossplatform equivalence. 

### **3.3.2** | **Other Technical Considerations** 

We noted that the intracellular markers (FoxP3, Granzyme B, and Eomes) exhibited relatively high background staining, which constrained the resolution of clearly defined positive populations. This nonspecific signal may arise from factors such as fixation/permeabilization artifacts or antibody crossreactivity with fixation- altered epitopes. The resolution of positive populations also varies depending on the instrument. Although these markers remained interpretable within the context of our gating strategy, further optimization such as evaluation of alternative fixation/permeabilization buffers or testing additional antibody clones could improve signal- tonoise ratios and strengthen the reproducibility of these readouts in future applications. 

In summary, we developed a 15- marker mass cytometry panel for the comprehensive characterization of the major leukocyte subsets within canine PBMCs. Importantly, the panel retains flexibility for future expansion through several unassigned metal channels, paving the way for future expansion. This OMIP provides a robust and scalable framework to support high- dimensional immune monitoring in canine models, thereby advancing translational research [1], improving the predictive power of preclinical safety and efficacy studies
